Farmland Loss Map

Taiwan and Malaysia: which fields disappeared, and which were built over

This map lays satellite-mapped changes in cropland over satellite imagery. The cropland data come from a global map that the University of Maryland made from Landsat images, comparing 2003 with 2019. Zoom in to a place you know and see what the orange and red land has become.

  • Green: cropland in 2019
  • Orange: cropland in 2003 but not in 2019 (it may have been fallow for more than four years, abandoned, planted with fruit trees or oil palm, or turned into fish ponds)
  • Red: the same, and built over with houses, factories or roads by 2021
  • Lime: oil palm plantations in 2019 (Johor only)
  • Dashed line: a park boundary, or the area the figures refer to

In this satellite map, “cropland” means herbaceous crops such as rice, vegetables and sugarcane; orchards, tea and oil palm are not counted. Click any county or state on the map to see its figures. The buttons at the top right of the map switch between satellite images from 1999, 2014, 2019 and today; press “Compare” and drag the line in the middle to see before and after.

Over the same period, 40,000 hectares were newly mapped as cropland (for example, land farmed again after a long fallow), so the total fell by only 13,000 hectares. Official statistics from the Ministry of Agriculture also show a decline: cultivated land fell from 803,000 hectares in 2012 to 775,000 hectares in 2025 (−3.4%). The two series cover different years, though. In the overlapping years 2012–2019 the official figure fell by 1.6%, while satellite-mapped cropland in 2019 was about 3% above its 2011 level. The official figure also includes orchards, tea gardens and long-term fallow land, and the satellite map undercounts small, fragmented fields, so the official total is much larger.

Orange does not necessarily mean buildings: the land may have been fallow for more than four years, abandoned, planted with fruit trees or turned into fish ponds. Red marks the places where the satellite data show something has been built.

Sources: Potapov et al. (2022) and Zanaga et al. (2022), computed for this page; Department of Statistics, Ministry of Agriculture (n.d.)

Places to look

  • Taichung City lost 17% of its cropland in 16 years, and about 40% of the land that stopped being cropland has been built over. Among counties and cities with more than 10,000 hectares of cropland, both figures are the highest. The largest red patch in the frame lies in the Taichung Park of the Central Taiwan Science Park (CTSP, established in 2003): within its boundary as mapped on OpenStreetMap, about 70% of the land was still cropland in 2003. At the Qixing site of the Houli Park to the north, the share is about 80%.

  • The dashed outline is the construction area of the Chiayi Science Park as mapped on OpenStreetMap. In 2019 the satellite data still classified about 90% of it as cropland. TSMC is building CoWoS and other advanced packaging plants here. Construction began in May 2024. Work on the first plant stopped after suspected archaeological remains were found, so the second plant was fast-tracked. By July 2026 the first completed plant was in mass production, and the science park’s second phase, about 90 hectares to the east, broke ground the same month.

In Malaysia, most of what this satellite map counts as cropland is rice, concentrated in Kedah, Perak, Perlis and Kelantan. In 16 years, 44,000 hectares stopped being cropland, but 45,000 hectares were newly mapped as cropland, so the total stayed about the same.

Johor has been called Malaysia’s “data centre capital”, and oil palm companies are setting aside large tracts of their land for data center industrial parks. Oil palm is planted once and grows for more than twenty years, so this cropland map leaves it out. To understand Malaysia, look at oil palm as well:

Official statistics: MPOB figures show that Malaysia’s oil palm area fell by 287,000 hectares (−4.9%) from 2019 to 2024. Johor lost 99,000 hectares (−13%), more than the whole land area of Singapore, and Selangor lost 21%. But MPOB’s 2025 figure for Johor rose again to 676,000 hectares (from 660,000 in 2024), so the size of the decline depends on which years you compare.

Satellite mapping: the lime areas on the map are where satellite data showed oil palm plantations in 2019 (Johor only). The satellite estimate for Johor is about 810,000 hectares; MPOB’s figure is 759,000 hectares, of which 694,000 were mature. The two are measured differently, so compare only their scale. Zoom in to southern Johor and compare with today’s imagery to see which plantations have become something else.

In 2019 the satellite data showed almost the whole site as large-scale oil palm plantation (lime).   • Among states with more than 10,000 hectares of cropland, Perlis lost the largest share (−22%), and the loss is concentrated around Chuping in the east. Much of this area was sugarcane. On about 4,500 hectares at Chuping, MSM stopped growing sugarcane and switched to rubber, oil palm and mango, until it ended those operations in 2019. Data and methods

Cropland

The global cropland map by the GLAD team at the University of Maryland (Potapov et al., 2022), classified from Landsat imagery at about 30 m resolution. “2003” means 2000–2003 and “2019” means 2016–2019; land counts as cropland if it was farmed during that period, with fallow of no more than four years. Cropland covers only annual and perennial herbaceous crops, not tree crops such as orchards, tea, oil palm or rubber. The authors also note that much of the cropland lost in Southeast Asia was converted to tree crops or aquaculture.

Built over

Classified with the European Space Agency’s ESA WorldCover 2021 (Zanaga et al., 2022; 10 m). Each 30 m pixel contains exactly nine 10 m pixels; if five or more of them are “built-up”, the pixel is drawn in red.

How areas are calculated

Pixel areas are adjusted for latitude (about 0.07 ha per pixel in Taiwan and 0.08 ha in Malaysia), then summed by county and state using the geoBoundaries administrative boundaries (Runfola et al., 2020). The Taiwan analysis excludes Penghu, Kinmen and Matsu. When the map is zoomed out, scattered pixels are drawn stronger than their true density so that they stay visible.

Oil palm

Oil palm plantations in 2019 come from Descals et al. (2021), classified from Sentinel satellite imagery at 10 m resolution. They include closed-canopy oil palm on both industrial and smallholder plantations; young palms whose canopy has not yet closed are not counted. This page shows Johor only. State figures use MPOB’s official statistics.

Satellite images by year

1999: NASA’s global annual Landsat composite (GWELD), served by NASA’s Global Imagery Browse Services (GIBS) at about 30 m resolution. Like the cropland data, it comes from Landsat. It shows the general pattern of fields, settlements and roads, but not individual buildings.

2014 and 2019: the February 2014 and December 2019 releases of Esri World Imagery Wayback. A release date is not a capture date; in some places the images were taken earlier.

Now: Google satellite imagery (Esri when Google cannot load), with shops, sights and transit icons hidden; turn off “Labels” to hide place names as well. Light: a light Google map that shows only administrative areas and place names, good for reading the overlay or taking screenshots (OpenStreetMap when Google cannot load). Images from any year may show clouds or seams.

Reading the map

  • Satellite classification has errors. The authors note that change is mapped less accurately than whether land was cropland in a given year, and that in places with small, mixed fields, such as much of Asia, the map underestimates cropland area.
  • “Newly mapped as cropland” includes land farmed again after a long fallow; it is not necessarily newly cleared farmland.
  • The map shows where changes overlap in space, not why they happened: why a given plot stopped being cropland has to be checked case by case. Neither country publishes official statistics on farmland converted for AI or semiconductors.
  • Basemap images were taken at different times in different places, mostly in recent years.
