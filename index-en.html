Research notes

Two research notes behind the map and the dashboard: a literature review that critically examines two competing explanations of Taiwan’s success, and an account of the spatial history of Malaysia’s technology industry. The abstract and the motivation are shown in full; click a heading to expand the rest. Both are translated from the Chinese originals.

Essay 01 · Literature Review & Framework

The Rise of Taiwan’s Semiconductor and AI Industries: The Developmental State, Global Production Networks, and the Prospects for Taiwan-Malaysia Cooperation

Literature review and policy framework · Wu Yun-Ching
Abstract

English-language scholarship offers two competing explanations for the rise of Taiwan’s chip and AI industries: the developmental state / industrial policy account (Wade, Amsden, Chu Wan-wen, Joseph Wong; the ITRI → UMC/TSMC spin-off model), and the firm-centered global production networks account (Henry Yeung’s strategic coupling; Langlois’s “vanishing hand” of vertical disintegration). The most defensible position is a synthesis: the state did play a decisive “midwife” role in the 1970s and 1980s, but Taiwan’s lasting dominance is inseparable from the contingent turn of the US industry toward a fabless/foundry division of labor in the 1980s and 1990s, and from Cold War access to the US market. This supports, in a qualified sense, the hypothesis of a coincidence in the transnational division of labor: contingency opened the window, and firm-level execution and previously accumulated state capacity let Taiwan pass through it.

Taiwan’s AI position is largely a derivative of its semiconductor position. Taiwan dominates AI hardware (TSMC held 69.9% of global foundry revenue in 2025; a near-monopoly in CoWoS advanced packaging; Foxconn holds about 40% of the global AI server market), but has no foundation models and no global platform companies: a severe imbalance between hardware and software. This risk of “hardware lock-in” has not yet been adequately theorized in the literature.

In principle, Malaysia and Taiwan are more complementary than competitive (Malaysia: about 13% of global back-end OSAT/ATP, land, water, cheaper power, an English-speaking workforce, ASEAN market access, geopolitical neutrality; Taiwan: advanced logic, advanced packaging, IC design, IP, ODM server integration). But the failure of SilTerra (from 1995) shows that the Taiwan model cannot be transplanted wholesale. The practical room for cooperation lies in mature-node fabs, OSAT/advanced packaging, and AI server and data center integration: yet these are exactly the areas where the two compete most fiercely.

Part 1: Drivers of Taiwan’s success identified in the literature

1.1 The developmental state classics (strong as interpretive evidence, contested as explanation)

The foundational works treat Taiwan (with South Korea) as the paradigm of the East Asian “developmental state”:

Robert Wade, Governing the Market: Economic Theory and the Role of Government in East Asian Industrialization (1990; revised editions 2003 and 2018, Princeton University Press). Wade rejects both the neoclassical “free market” reading and a crude “statist” one, arguing that Taiwan is a “governed market”: the state guides investment and selectively protects strategic industries, while still relying on market mechanisms.

Alice H. Amsden, The Rise of “The Rest”: Challenges to the West from Late-Industrializing Economies (2001, Oxford University Press). Catching up is essentially the learning and absorption of technology, enforced by a “reciprocal control mechanism”: subsidies are conditional on performance standards (export targets, local-content ratios). Amsden distinguishes an “independent” path (Taiwan and Korea, building national firms that invest in R&D) from an “integrationist” path (Latin America, led by multinationals).

Alice H. Amsden & Wan-wen Chu, Beyond Late Development: Taiwan’s Upgrading Policies (2003, MIT Press). Extends the argument to “latecomer” upgrading in mature high-tech sectors, where the key is “skills, speed and scale”, with government laboratories (ITRI) paving the way for private mass production.

Peter B. Evans, Embedded Autonomy: States and Industrial Transformation (1995, Princeton University Press). Proposes an influential typology of state roles (custodian, demiurge, midwife, husbandry) and the concept of “embedded autonomy”: a bureaucracy that is autonomous yet closely connected to private capital. This typology underpins the later argument that the Taiwanese state was closer to a midwife (inducing private firms into new sectors) than a demiurge (the state producing directly): in contrast to Korea’s husbandry of the chaebol.

1.2 The ITRI spin-off model (strong evidence, fully documented empirically)

This is the empirical backbone of the industrial policy account:

  • ITRI (founded in 1973) is described as a “technology intermediary” linking foreign laboratories and local firms.
  • The 1976 RCA technology transfer agreement (traceable to a 1974 breakfast meeting of technocrats, including K. T. Li’s circle and RCA’s Wen-Yuan Pan) produced Taiwan’s first IC plant.
  • ITRI spun off UMC (1980) and TSMC (1987), transferring plant, equipment, technology and 98 professionals, as well as Taiwan Mask (1989).
  • Hsinchu Science Park (1980) concentrated firms, suppliers and two anchor universities (National Chiao Tung and National Tsing Hua), and offered subsidized land, water and power and low taxes.
  • The Philips joint venture was decisive: Morris Chang approached more than ten IC companies, and only Philips responded, taking a stake and providing technology and a patent umbrella that anchored TSMC’s credibility.
  • ECIPE’s analysis (2024) adds a useful detail: the Taiwan model evolved from “state-led creation” to “state-enabled upgrading”, deliberately avoiding permanent state ownership and pushing firms to face the test of the market.

1.3 The pure-play foundry business model (strong evidence)

Morris Chang’s insight, a specialist foundry that only manufactures other companies’ designs and has no products of its own, is consistently seen in the literature as the key move that catalyzed the global fabless ecosystem. Chang and the founding generation of Taiwan’s tech leadership mostly held US degrees (MIT, Stanford) and had worked at Texas Instruments, Bell Labs and IBM, filling TSMC with US-trained managers.

1.4 Human capital and “brain circulation” (strong evidence)

AnnaLee Saxenian & Jinn-Yuh Hsu, “The Silicon Valley–Hsinchu Connection: Technical Communities and Industrial Upgrading,” Industrial and Corporate Change 10(4): 893–920 (2001); Saxenian, “From Brain Drain to Brain Circulation,” Studies in Comparative International Development 40(2): 35–61 (2005); Saxenian, Regional Advantage (1994, Harvard UP).

Core claim: between Silicon Valley and Hsinchu there was two-way circulation, not a one-way “brain drain”. Saxenian notes that more than half of the companies in Hsinchu Science Park were founded by returnees from Silicon Valley, and that Taiwanese technocrats who had lived in the US steered policy toward a market-oriented (rather than classically statist) model, including the venture capital industry created in 1985.

Debate: Zhou & Hsu (2008, Pacific Affairs) show the limits of this model: brain circulation was not replicated in Beijing’s Zhongguancun, because its preconditions (decentralized firm networks, trust-based learning between firms, venture capital infrastructure, state facilitation of technology transfer) were absent. This bears directly on the transferability question in Part 4.

1.5 Venture capital, tax incentives and SME networks (strong evidence)

Taiwan built a venture capital industry from 1985 (early for Asia) and used the 20-year R&D tax incentives of the Statute for Upgrading Industries (1991). The literature on SME networks and flexible production (Gary Hamilton, Ian Skoggard) stresses Taiwan’s dense subcontracting networks, though this account fits the PC and components era better than the capital-intensive foundry era.

1.6 The AI industry: a hardware supply-chain story (strong evidence on hardware, contested on value capture)

Taiwan’s AI position rests on the physical AI stack:

  • Foundry: TSMC held 69.9% of global foundry revenue for full-year 2025 (US$122.54bn) and 70.4% in Q4 2025 (TrendForce, reported by the Taipei Times on March 14, 2026), up from 64.4% in 2024; advanced nodes (3nm, 2nm) are highly concentrated in Taiwan.
  • Advanced packaging: CoWoS is the bottleneck for Nvidia/AMD GPUs; TSMC’s CoWoS capacity is projected to grow from about 70,000 to 115,000 wafers a month over 2025–26 (industry estimates, TechNews), with ASE, SPIL, KYEC and others on the OSAT side.
  • ODMs and server assembly: Foxconn (about 40% of the global AI server market; chairman Young Liu at the shareholders’ meeting on May 29, 2025), Quanta/QCT, Wistron, Wiwynn (spun off from Wistron), Inventec, Pegatron and Compal, plus thermal (AVC, Auras), power (Delta, Lite-On), networking (Accton) and substrates (Unimicron, Kinsus). In Q1 2026, the five largest ODMs had combined revenue of over NT$2 trillion (about US$63bn) (TVBS/DIGITIMES).
  • National strategy: the Taiwan AI Action Plan and the draft Artificial Intelligence Basic Act.

Scholarly assessment: commentators increasingly see Taiwan’s AI role as a derivative of its semiconductor position, with Taiwan controlling almost the entire back end of AI hardware. This is both a finding and a research gap (see Part 3).

Part 2: Critical and revisionist readings: assessing the contingency hypothesis

The core hypothesis: Taiwan’s success was essentially a contingent outcome of the global division of labor, rather than mainly the work of industrial policy. This finds partial support in a substantial and rigorous body of scholarship.

2.1 Yeung’s strategic coupling: the strongest structural challenge (strong evidence, highly influential)

Henry Wai-chung Yeung, Strategic Coupling: East Asian Industrial Transformation in the New Global Economy (2016, Cornell University Press) and Interconnected Worlds: Global Electronics and Production Networks in East Asia (2022, Stanford University Press).

Core argument: since the late 1980s, East Asia’s lead firms (TSMC, Samsung, Foxconn) have outgrown their home states and no longer depend on state support. Industrial transformation has therefore been driven by firm-specific strategies and “strategic coupling”, that is, embedding in global production networks orchestrated by lead firms (Apple, Nvidia, Qualcomm), rather than by state direction. In Yeung’s view, the performance of national firms cannot be read as a direct result of state action, and the developmental state is a necessary but not sufficient condition.

This is the single most important scholarly source for the contingency argument, because it reframes success as coupling with global value chains orchestrated by lead firms: that is, as securing a favorable position in the global division of labor.

2.2 Vertical disintegration and the “vanishing hand” literature (strong evidence, directly supports the contingency argument)

Langlois & Steinmueller, “The Evolution of Competitive Advantage in the Global Semiconductor Industry, 1947–1996,” in Mowery & Nelson (eds.), The Sources of Industrial Leadership (1999, Cambridge UP); Richard Langlois, “The Vanishing Hand,” Industrial and Corporate Change (2003); Macher, Mowery et al. (2002); Kapoor on complementors; Langlois & Robertson, Research Policy 21: 297–313 (1992) on modularity.

The fabless/foundry split was a shift in industrial architecture that originated in the United States, driven by modular design, rising fab costs, and the dynamics of transactions and capabilities. About 250 fabless start-ups were born in Silicon Valley between 1985 and 1994; by 2002, 475 of the world’s 640 fabless firms were in the US. Yeung (2022) explicitly documents this “fabless revolution” as an enabling condition for TSMC.

Implications for the paper: the timing of the US fabless shift was essentially exogenous to Taiwanese policy. TSMC did not create the demand for the fabless model; it positioned itself to capture demand that the US industrial architecture would have generated anyway. This strongly supports the “windfall” reading, with the crucial caveat that Taiwan had already (through ITRI/RCA) built the capacity to respond.

2.3 Cold War developmentalism and US aid (strong evidence; the exogeneity debate is genuinely unsettled)

US economic aid to Taiwan from 1950 to 1965 totaled about US$1.4bn (about US$16bn in today’s money by some estimates), roughly 15 to 25% of all US economic aid to the “Far East”; there was also more than US$3bn in military aid, and land reform. Key sources: Neil Jacoby, U.S. Aid to Taiwan (1966); the Amsden and Cumings debate over endogenous and exogenous factors; Meredith Woo-Cumings (ed.), The Developmental State (1999); Stephan Haggard, Pathways from the Periphery (1990).

Amsden herself acknowledges that US aid sustained the regime in its first years, and that holding Taiwan up as a model of capitalist development for other poor countries is futile: an admission of the case’s particularity that bears directly on transferability. The KMT state’s capacity, inherited from mainland technocrats, and privileged access to the US market are seen as windfalls unavailable to other latecomers.

2.4 Industrial policy failures: evidence of selection and survivorship bias (strong evidence)

This is the strongest empirical case against the “wise state” narrative:

  • The DRAM collapse: the memory makers of the “Two Trillion, Twin Stars” program (ProMOS, Powerchip, Nanya and others) were overtaken first by Korea and then by China; the rescue through the state-backed Taiwan Memory Company (TMC) failed in November 2009, when the Legislative Yuan rejected a NT$5bn injection from the National Development Fund (IEEE Spectrum). The industry absorbed more than NT$1 trillion without developing its own technology.
  • Flat panels: the other “star”; AUO and Innolux (the “twin tigers”) never recovered after 2008, beaten by Korean and Chinese makers (CommonWealth Magazine, 2014).
  • Solar photovoltaics: a similar cycle of boom and bust.

Implication: the same state, with the same tools (subsidies, the National Development Fund, science parks), applied them to DRAM, panels and solar, and failed. This is strong evidence that foundry success is over-attributed to policy and reflects survivorship bias: “It was not policy that picked TSMC; TSMC picked itself.” The divergence between DRAM and the foundries (documented in CommonWealth’s article on the “Taiwan semiconductor paradox”) is an ideal within-case comparison.

2.5 Econometric and quantitative assessments (thin but growing)

Yang, Huang & Hou, “Tax incentives and R&D activity: Firm-level evidence from Taiwan,” Research Policy 41(9): 1578–1588 (2012): uses propensity-score matching to correct for selection bias in the R&D investment credits of the Statute for Upgrading Industries.

RIETI Discussion Paper 25-E-005 (2025), “Science Parks in Taiwan and Their Value-adding Contributions”: using augmented inverse probability weighting and a staggered difference-in-differences design, it finds positive additionality (R&D, PhD hiring, TFP, patent quality) in the Hsinchu, Central and Southern Taiwan science parks.

The broader literature, such as David, Hall & Toole, Research Policy 29 (2000), warns that subsidies are not allocated at random (selection bias). Gap: there is no rigorous counterfactual estimate of the return on the flagship intervention itself, ITRI → foundry.

2.6 Counter-critiques defending the industrial policy account (strong evidence)

Chu Wan-wen: “Can Taiwan’s Second Movers Upgrade via Branding?” Research Policy 38(6): 1054–1065 (2009); “Latecomer Upgrading in Taiwan,” Journal of the Asia Pacific Economy 20(3): 369–384 (2015); “Catch-up and Learning in Taiwan,” in Oqubay & Ohno (eds.), How Nations Learn (2019, Oxford UP). Chu argues that industrial policy adapted to each stage drove Taiwan’s growth, against market-centered readings.

Joseph Wong, Betting on Biotech: Innovation and the Limits of Asia’s Developmental State (2011, Cornell UP): importantly, Wong shows the model’s limits. Under the fundamental uncertainty (rather than manageable risk) of frontier biotech, Taiwan, Korea and Singapore all struggled to “pick winners”. This refines the developmental state account rather than simply defending it.

Douglas Fuller, Paper Tigers, Hidden Dragons: Firms and the Political Economy of China’s Technological Development (2016/2019, Oxford UP): shows that China’s vast statist push largely failed, while “hybrid” firms with ethnic Chinese management and foreign financing succeeded. This implicitly warns against reading Taiwan as a purely state victory, and puts firms’ financing structure and commitment above state largesse: a bridge between the developmental state and GPN camps.

2.7 The “silicon shield” literature (strong evidence; contested interpretation)

Chris Miller, Chip War: The Fight for the World’s Most Critical Technology (2022); Craig Addison (who coined “silicon shield”); Kathrin Hille (reporting for the FT). Tsai Ing-wen framed chips as a silicon shield that lets Taiwan protect itself.

Debate: concentration as security (deterrence: destroying the prize deters invasion) versus concentration as vulnerability (a single point of failure that invites coercion or a targeted attack). Miller notes that the shield cuts both ways. Post-2018 US export controls, the CHIPS Act, TSMC’s fabs in Arizona and Japan, supply-chain fragmentation and the techno-nationalism of “a real nation-state should have fabs” (Yeung 2022) have sharpened the debate.

Assessment of Part 2: the evidence substantially supports a qualified contingency thesis. The restructuring of the global division of labor (the fabless shift) and Cold War geopolitics were necessary and largely exogenous conditions. But pure contingency goes too far: ITRI/RCA capability-building and the Philips anchor were deliberate state acts that let Taiwan seize the window. The honest formulation: structural contingency created the opportunity; state midwifery and firm-level strategic coupling realized it. And the failures in DRAM, panels and solar show that the state could not manufacture success at will: in itself the strongest evidence against the strong-policy account and for the contingency reading.

Part 3: Research gaps: where the literature is thin

3.1 Labor (near-total silence in the mainstream classics)

The developmental state and GPN literatures are strikingly silent on fab working conditions, working hours, migrant workers in electronics supply chains, the gender composition of fab operators and occupational health. Where these are documented, it is in science and technology studies (STS) and legal scholarship, not in political economy.

The RCA Taiwan case: RCA ran three plants in Taiwan from 1970 to 1992, employing about 80,000 people at its peak and exposing a largely young, female workforce to a cocktail of 31 toxic substances, including trichloroethylene and benzene (SCMP). On January 15, 2025, Taiwan’s High Court ordered RCA, GE, Technicolor and affiliates to pay NT$170 million (about US$5.14 million) to 222 plaintiffs (CNA/Focus Taiwan). Key scholarship: Paul Jobin, Chen Hsin-hsing and Lin Yi-ping in East Asian Science, Technology and Society (2011; 2023). This is the largest occupational and toxic tort case in Taiwan’s history, yet it is almost absent from the industrial policy narratives that celebrate the same era. This erasure is itself a finding.

3.2 Environment and resources (under-theorized)

The water, power, land, PFAS and carbon footprint of fabs; the political economy of subsidizing energy for semiconductor and AI expansion; the tension between Taiwan’s nuclear-free homeland policy and surging power demand from AI and fabs. Technical and EHS literatures exist, but few studies bring critical political economy into the developmental state debate.

3.3 Space and urban and regional planning (the clearest comparative advantage: a real gap)

Land expropriation for science parks (enclosure), soaring housing prices in Hsinchu and Tainan, regional inequality, commuting burdens and the urban impacts of fab expansion are almost unstudied in the English-language political economy literature. For someone trained in urban planning, this is the most defensible space for producing original primary data.

3.4 The AI industry itself (methodologically immature)

  • Measurement: how to define and measure success in the “AI industry”: output versus captured value.
  • Value capture versus assembly: does Taiwan capture value, or merely assemble? The gap between the single-digit gross margins of server ODMs and TSMC’s foundry margins reveals a stark internal asymmetry.
  • Hardware-software asymmetry: Taiwan has no foundation models and no platform companies: Taiwan builds the AI factories but does not own the AI. The trap of “hardware lock-in” is asserted in commentary but not rigorously theorized.

3.5 Comparative gaps

Despite their obvious analytical value, rigorous comparisons between Taiwan and Southeast Asian latecomers (Malaysia, Vietnam, Thailand) are rare: exactly the space Part 4 can fill.

3.6 Methodological gaps

Heavy reliance on firm interviews and case studies of winners (survivorship bias: Yeung himself drew on more than 100 executive interviews). Longitudinal firm-level microdata and counterfactual or quasi-experimental designs are scarce (the 2012 Research Policy PSM study and the 2025 RIETI difference-in-differences are exceptions, not the norm).

3.7 Theoretical gaps

How to reconcile the agency of the developmental state with the structural contingency of GVCs and GPNs; luck, conjuncture and path dependence are under-theorized in theories of late development. A possible contribution: explicitly model contingency (the fabless window) as a structural opportunity, and coupling and state midwifery as agency under that opportunity.

Part 4: Taiwan and Malaysia compared, and future cooperation

4.1 Malaysia’s technology trajectory (strong evidence)

Penang set up a free trade zone in 1972, attracting Intel (1972), AMD, Hewlett-Packard, Osram and Clarion: electronics FDI that began earlier than Taiwan’s foundry era. Malaysia became a global back-end hub: as of 2023 it accounted for about 13% of global assembly, packaging and test (ATP) output and was the world’s sixth-largest semiconductor exporter (MIDA).

Rajah Rasiah’s body of work (e.g., Development and Change 1992; UNU-MERIT working papers; Best & Rasiah) is indispensable English-language literature: Penang deepened its capabilities (R&D mandates, local suppliers of equipment and test systems), yet remained MNC-driven with thin local R&D, constrained by federal coordination failures and a restrictive immigration policy that blocks the inflow of high-tech talent. Complementary sources: Hal Hill, K.S. Jomo, Greg Felker, Wong Chan-Yuan; Yeung’s GPN research on Malaysia.

The core puzzle: why did Malaysia, with an earlier start in electronics FDI, fail to move upstream into wafer fabrication, while Taiwan succeeded? Rasiah’s answer: “truncated” MNC-led operations, weak formation of local firms and systemic coordination failures, in contrast with Taiwan’s state-built local capabilities and networks of returnee engineers.

4.2 SilTerra: the key case of failed transplantation (strong evidence)

SilTerra (founded in November 1995 as Wafer Technology Malaysia; renamed in 1999; a national project of Mahathir; first wafers in 2000) is the most instructive comparison in the transferability argument.

Why it failed: persistent losses; a “revolving door” of CEOs; and, most critically, an underdeveloped local IC design industry that could not generate enough foundry orders (by contrast, TSMC’s demand came from the US fabless ecosystem, not from domestic designers). Khazanah sold it in 2021 to DNeX (60%) and China Growth Partners Fund (40%). It never moved beyond 200mm and mature nodes.

SilTerra versus TSMC is the anchor case study for transferability: the same ambition (pure-play foundry), opposite outcomes. The decisive difference was not state will but whether a customer base of already-coupled global lead firms existed, together with prior accumulation of capabilities: a direct confirmation of the structural reading of Yeung and Langlois over a voluntarist policy reading.

4.3 Malaysia’s current push (strong evidence; results yet to be seen)

  • The National Semiconductor Strategy (NSS), launched by Prime Minister Anwar at SEMICON SEA on May 28, 2024: at least RM25bn (about US$5.3bn) in fiscal support; three phases over ten years; targets to train and upskill 60,000 engineers, build at least 10 design and advanced packaging champions (each with RM1bn to RM4.7bn in revenue), and attract RM500bn in investment (The Edge Malaysia; MIDA; Digital News Asia). Phase 1 upgrades OSAT to advanced packaging; fabs are deferred to Phase 3 (trade minister Zafrul said Malaysia cannot afford them yet).
  • Penang attracted a record RM61bn in semiconductor FDI in 2023, more than the previous seven years combined, including Intel’s RM30bn advanced packaging plant (Digital News Asia). Infineon has pledged RM25bn (silicon carbide, Kulim).
  • Johor’s data center boom: YTL’s US$4.3bn AI infrastructure deal with Nvidia (US$2.4bn invested, 200MW built); Johor is expected to hold about 60% of national data center capacity by 2030; the Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone (January 2025); Malaysian industrial power costs about US$0.10 per kWh, against about US$0.27 in Singapore.
  • An ARM licensing deal (2025); a National AI Office (set up at the end of 2024); MyDIGITAL and the Ministry of Digital; the goal of becoming an “AI nation” by 2030.
  • US export controls: on July 14, 2025, Malaysia introduced strategic trade permits for US-origin advanced AI chips (Directive 1/2025), as the US scrutinized Malaysia as a transshipment hub (the fraud case in Singapore involving DeepSeek; draft US licensing rules targeting Malaysia and Thailand). This is an immediate vulnerability for Malaysia’s data center strategy.

4.4 Structural constraints (strong evidence)

Talent: Wong Siew Hai of the Malaysia Semiconductor Industry Association (MSIA) says Malaysia loses about 15% of its talent each year to Singapore, Taiwan, the US and Europe, with wages inflating while the talent pool does not grow; Shafiq Kadir of CGS International says higher education is ill-prepared to produce graduates with the right skill sets.

The effects of Bumiputera policy on human capital formation and firm creation; the dominance of government-linked companies (GLCs); reliance on MNCs rather than local champions; the ringgit and wage structures; low local R&D intensity; a broken pipeline in technical and vocational education (TVET); policy discontinuity across governments.

Critiques of data center jobs: Sara Loo of ISEAS and others warn that job promises may be illusory: a facility needs only 50 to 200 staff, as the machines mostly run themselves. Water and power stress in Johor (in 2019, 250,000 people in Johor faced water shortages) is a sustainability risk.

4.5 Taiwanese firms in Malaysia (strong evidence)

Foxconn and DNeX: an MoU signed in May 2022 to build a 12-inch fab at 28/40nm with 40,000 wafers a month (estimated at US$3–5bn); Foxconn (through Big Innovation Holdings) holds about 5% of DNeX and a board seat, and through it indirect exposure to SilTerra. More broadly, China+1 and friend-shoring dynamics; ties with ASE/Aemulus, Inari Amertron, Vanguard International and Nanya; Malaysia actively courting Taiwanese investors.

Vectors of complementarity (practical): mature-node fabs serving automotive, IoT and connectivity; joint development in OSAT and advanced packaging; AI server and data center integration that combines Malaysian power with Taiwanese ODM know-how; localization of equipment and test systems.

Competitive collisions (to be stated plainly): the two compete exactly where they overlap: mature-node fabs, back-end packaging and data centers. Phase 1 of Malaysia’s NSS (upgrading OSAT to advanced packaging) targets the turf of ASE, SPIL and KYEC. Any policy brief must make this tension explicit.

4.7 Policy learning and the transferability question (strong evidence; the honest core of Part 4)

What Malaysia can reasonably learn from Taiwan: ITRI’s “technology intermediary” model (Malaysia’s MIMOS is the counterpart, but under-resourced); the cluster model of science parks; technology transfer through licensing; building a local IC design demand base before investing in fabs (the lesson of SilTerra); the disciplined, spin-off-oriented role of the National Development Fund.

What cannot be transplanted (facing the transferability question): the Taiwan model relied on (i) Cold War US aid, market access and security guarantees; (ii) a particular authoritarian-era configuration of state and society, with the capacity of mainlander technocrats; (iii) the exogenous US fabless window of the 1980s and 1990s; and (iv) the diaspora network of Chinese engineers in Silicon Valley (Saxenian’s brain circulation). Malaysia has none of these. The literature on policy mobility, and the record of repeated failures of the “Taiwan model” elsewhere (SilTerra in Malaysia, India’s stalled fab attempts, Vietnam), confirm that institutions travel poorly. Zhou & Hsu (2008) on the failed replication of brain circulation in Beijing is the theoretical anchor here.

What Taiwan can learn from Malaysia (the underrated reverse flow): availability of energy and land; ASEAN diplomatic space and non-alignment (a hedge Taiwan structurally lacks); openness to foreign talent and an English-language environment: all areas where Taiwan faces hard constraints (power adequacy under the nuclear-free policy; diplomatic isolation; talent shortages; language barriers for foreign talent).

Recommendations and caveats

Recommendations (for the paper and a policy brief)

Phase 1: framing (now). Organize the paper’s literature review around the debate between the developmental state and GPN/strategic coupling. Adopt an explicit synthetic framework: Evans’s midwife role + Yeung’s strategic coupling + Langlois’s structural window, formalized as “structural contingency creates the opportunity; state midwifery and firm coupling realize it”. Use the DRAM, panel and solar failures as within-case comparisons to show that the state cannot manufacture success at will: the single strongest argument for the contingency thesis.

Phase 2: original contribution (core). Stake the novel claims on the gaps in labor, environment and urban space (§3.1 to 3.3): here an urban planning background is decisive, and the literature is weakest. Produce primary data on land expropriation, housing prices and commuting in Hsinchu and Tainan: the most defensible novelty, and a direct answer to the methodological gap (§3.6).

Phase 3: the comparative payload. Use SilTerra versus TSMC as the anchor comparison for transferability, with the Taiwan-Malaysia complementarity and competition matrix (§4.6) at the core of the policy brief. State the competitive collisions (mature-node fabs, packaging, data centers) plainly, rather than offering only a win-win frame.

Indicators that would change the assessment

  • If Malaysia’s NSS produces at least one globally competitive local design or advanced packaging champion before 2030, the thesis of MNC dependence and a middle-income trap would be substantially weakened.
  • If TSMC’s overseas fabs (Arizona, Japan, Germany) reach advanced-node parity with Taiwan, both the logic of the “silicon shield” and the premise of concentration in Taiwan would weaken.
  • If US export controls on AI chips to Malaysia tighten into a formal licensing regime, the Johor data center thesis would be materially damaged: a variable to track.
  • If a rigorous counterfactual firm-level evaluation of foundry-era interventions appears, the debate on policy effectiveness might finally be settled; until then, it remains structurally unresolved.

Caveats

Source quality varies by part. Parts 1 and 2 rest on peer-reviewed books and journals (Wade, Amsden, Evans, Yeung, Chu Wan-wen, Fuller, Saxenian, Langlois; Research Policy, Industrial and Corporate Change) and can be cited academically. The material on the AI supply chain and current affairs in Malaysia comes largely from industry and media sources (DIGITIMES, CommonWealth, The Edge Malaysia, Forbes, MIDA, TrendForce). Market shares, capacities and FDI figures should be treated as indicative and specific to 2025–26, and re-checked against primary statistics (SEMI, Ministry of Economic Affairs, MIDA, company filings) before publication.

Some figures are projections rather than realized outcomes (Johor’s ~60% of capacity by 2030; the CoWoS ramp; the NSS targets). They should be labeled as forward-looking statements.

The central question of contingency versus policy cannot be settled empirically without a counterfactual design: the paper should offer a reasoned interpretation, not claim causal proof.

The RCA figures (about 80,000 employees at peak; 31 substances; the more than 1,300 cancer cases cited in early reports, against 222 plaintiffs in the January 2025 judgment) come from different stages of a two-decade litigation involving several groups of plaintiffs: cite the specific judgment and plaintiff group precisely to avoid confusion.

Essay 02 · Research Background

The Trajectory of Malaysia’s Technology Industry: State Spatial Restructuring and Its Historical Convergence with Global AI Capital

Research background and literature review · Gan Sin Tiong
Motivation

Looking back over half a century of Malaysia’s technology industry, one can clearly see a trajectory in which the state seeks to connect with global capital through state spatial restructuring. It runs from the free trade zone set up in Penang in the 1970s, which drew Intel and other American firms and built a remarkably resilient “Silicon Valley of the East”; through the late 1990s, when the Mahathir government threw the weight of the state behind a top-down effort to build Cyberjaya, envisioned as an “Asian Silicon Valley”, under the Multimedia Super Corridor (MSC); to Iskandar Malaysia in Johor, launched in 2006 and now Southeast Asia’s most closely watched cluster of AI data centers. For decades, Malaysia has used this connected series of urban mega-projects to show the world its strong ambition to become a “world-class player”.

Paradoxically, however, the scholarly literature of the past few decades has been broadly pessimistic and critical about Malaysia’s technology industry. Most studies see this model of development, heavily dependent on foreign direct investment (FDI), as essentially “dependent industrialization”, which did little to indigenize technology and left Malaysia stuck for years in low-value-added contract manufacturing. Yet reality seems to diverge sharply from these pessimistic expectations. At a moment when the global AI industry is booming and countries are competing fiercely for advanced technology infrastructure, Malaysia, once criticized for lacking an environment for homegrown innovation, has in fact drawn global tech giants such as Microsoft, Google and Nvidia, arriving with billions of dollars in capital.

This raises a question. Do past theories have blind spots in explaining today’s flows of technology capital? Or are these AI giants not interested in Malaysia’s “capacity for technological innovation” at all, but in the particular spatial and resource advantages that Malaysia has accumulated through years of spatial restructuring? This is the central puzzle this study seeks to unravel. We need to re-examine the development of Malaysia’s technology industry within the spatial logic of global technology capital, asking how the state responds to the demands of international capital through spatial restructuring, and how this is reshaping Malaysia’s real position in the global AI value chain.

2. Literature review

To make sense of Malaysia’s contradictory position in today’s AI boom, we must first return to the scholarship’s historical critique of its “dependent development” and its “spatial projects”.

Early political economy and innovation systems research on Malaysia’s technological catch-up focused mostly on the “disconnect between foreign capital and the local economy”. The classic studies of Jomo (1993) and Rasiah (1995) long ago pointed out that the electronics miracle Malaysia built in Penang was, to a degree, an “enclave economy”. The multinationals brought jobs and equipment, but core R&D and design stayed in their home countries. Comparing the catch-up paths of the four Asian tigers, Hobday (1995) also stated bluntly that whereas Taiwan succeeded in translating technology to local firms through national research institutes, Malaysia’s national innovation system was structurally deficient. This lack of spin-off mechanisms and technology spillovers kept Malaysian firms at the bottom of the value chain for a long time (Rasiah, 2010; Wong, 2011). In this context, scholarly pessimism about Malaysia’s technology industry stems mainly from its persistent failure to build a self-sustaining local innovation system.

Faced with this bottleneck in technological upgrading, the Malaysian state has often answered with ever grander spatial restructuring and mega-projects. In his critical study of Cyberjaya, Bunnell (2002) sharply observes that the government was in thrall to a myth of “techno-utopia”, believing that heavy state investment in hard infrastructure and the designation of science parks would naturally breed a software innovation culture like Silicon Valley’s. In practice, this top-down model of spatial development, detached from local social context, often reduced Cyberjaya to a property development or a hub for multinationals’ outsourced low-end back-office work, with little real technological upgrading. By contrast, Hutchinson (2009) argues that Penang retained a degree of cluster resilience because its state government showed bottom-up institutional flexibility in attracting investment and linking industry with academia, rather than relying simply on the federal government’s mega-scale spatial designations.

When this critical lens is extended to today’s wave of AI, some patterns emerge. Recent studies such as Kam (2025) and Othman (2025) note that Malaysia still faces serious gaps in high-level talent and technology as it confronts the new expansion of AI chips and data centers. Lee’s (2021) empirical analysis also cautions that local semiconductor firms trying to move into AI chip design still face patent barriers that are hard to overcome. In other words, the core structural problems that worried scholars, “dependent development” and a lack of innovation capacity, have not really been solved.

If technological capability has not changed in kind, why is transnational AI capital still pouring in? This may point to the particular spatial needs of capital accumulation in the AI era. AI-era infrastructure (such as hyperscale data centers) may no longer need intensive R&D brainpower so much as vast amounts of land, stable and cheap water and power, and a relatively neutral geopolitical safe haven. The national spatial restructuring that Malaysia carried out over decades to build “world-class” technology parks (such as Cyberjaya’s grid hinterland and the vast hinterland of Iskandar Malaysia) has unexpectedly become the ideal spatial container for these “power-hungry, water-hungry” technology behemoths. This also suggests that the “dividend” Malaysia now enjoys from incoming AI data centers may come not from overcoming the dilemmas of technological catch-up, but from a historical, contingent convergence between transnational capital searching for a new “spatial fix” and Malaysia’s existing urban mega-projects.

3. The development of Malaysia’s technology industry

The development of Malaysia’s technology industry is a history of state-led spatial planning that took in transnational capital and repeatedly tried to climb the global value chain. Its trajectory falls into four key periods: the foundation of the “enclave economy” in the 1970s, the “techno-utopia” experiment of the 1990s, the growing pains of upgrading after the millennium, and today’s new era of AI and restructured computing power. Drawing on scholarship, official policy and institutional reports, what follows traces the history and trends of Malaysia’s technology industry.

1970s–1980s: Free trade zones and the rise of the “Silicon Valley of the East”

Malaysia’s technology industry did not begin with the endogenous evolution of local technology, but with an export-oriented industrialization policy adopted to deal with high unemployment and economic crisis. In 1972, led by the federal government and Penang’s Chief Minister Lim Chong Eu, Malaysia’s first free trade zone (FTZ) was set up. With tax exemptions, good infrastructure and cheap labor, the Penang Development Corporation (PDC) drew eight multinational tech companies, including Intel and AMD, known as the “Eight Samurai” (Hutchinson, 2009). The economists Jomo (1993) and Rasiah (1995) sharply criticized this model as an “enclave economy”. The multinationals brought jobs and export figures, but without mandatory technology transfer clauses their core R&D stayed at home, leaving a serious technological gap between foreign capital and local SMEs. In this period Malaysia secured a foothold in the back end of the global semiconductor industry, outsourced assembly and test (OSAT), but also planted the risk of over-reliance on FDI.

1990s–2020s: The Multimedia Super Corridor and attempts at a “national innovation system”

Facing the rise of the internet in the late 1990s and the technological catch-up of the four Asian tigers, the Mahathir government realized that hardware contract manufacturing alone could not sustain competitiveness, and launched top-down urban mega-projects. The government designated the Multimedia Super Corridor (MSC) and spent heavily on Cyberjaya, aimed at becoming an “Asian Silicon Valley”, in an attempt to shift the industry from hardware manufacturing toward software and multimedia innovation. Bunnell (2002) strongly criticized this model of spatial development, detached from local social context and built on the belief that hard infrastructure brings software innovation, which ultimately failed to breed homegrown software giants. Meanwhile, although the government set up MIMOS (the Malaysian Institute of Microelectronic Systems) to promote local IC design, Wong (2011) points out serious disconnects between industry, academia and research in Malaysia’s national innovation system (NIS). Lacking a mechanism like Taiwan’s ITRI for turning technology into spin-offs, Malaysia met serious structural setbacks when it tried to move up into upstream R&D (Hobday, 1995).

In the 2010s, the rise of China’s “red supply chain” put heavy pressure on Malaysia’s low-end contract manufacturing; yet the US-China trade and technology wars that followed unexpectedly brought Malaysia orders shifted under “China+1”. Although Penang’s local supply chain made breakthroughs in manufacturing automation equipment, the industry as a whole still faced severe bottlenecks in technological upgrading. Lee (2021) warns, on empirical grounds, that local firms face huge patent barriers in moving into high-end chip design. Worse, Hutchinson & Lee (2026) point out that with stagnant wage structures, Malaysia is losing much of its high-level STEM talent to the pull of Singapore, seriously weakening its R&D potential.

2024 to today: An AI computing hub and the National Semiconductor Strategy

As the world enters an arms race in generative AI and large language models, Malaysia’s technology industry has set out on two very different but mutually reinforcing new trajectories.

Policy in the lead: the National Semiconductor Strategy (NSS) pushes for high-end manufacturing. According to the blueprint released by the Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry (MITI) and CREST, the Malaysian government allocated US$5.3bn in 2024, vowing to move beyond a pure contract manufacturing role. Policy now centers on attracting foreign investment in advanced packaging and high-bandwidth memory (HBM) lines. The government is also assembling a local “fabless consortium”, using sovereign funds to invest in deep-tech start-ups and fill a decades-long gap in IC design (CREST, 2024; Othman, 2025).

A new arena of spatial restructuring: Southeast Asia’s multibillion-dollar AI data centers. The vast hinterlands once reserved for software parks (such as Cyberjaya) and Johor’s Iskandar Malaysia, launched in 2006, have unexpectedly become ideal containers for AI computing infrastructure. With relatively cheap water and power, abundant land and its advantage as a “neutral haven” in geopolitics, Malaysia has drawn giants such as Microsoft, Google and Nvidia (Fourteau, 2025). But this has also brought serious challenges for land and resources. Official data and research (MIDA, 2025; Kam, 2025) both point out that these data centers, dubbed “power- and water-hungry monsters”, are creating competition with household water use and a risk of overloading the national grid. To meet foreign investors’ demand for green power (RE100), the government introduced the Corporate Renewable Energy Supply Scheme (CRESS), which lets data centers buy green power directly from private producers. Future technology parks will be pushed toward high water-use efficiency (WUE) and closed-loop green energy designs (PETRA, 2025).
