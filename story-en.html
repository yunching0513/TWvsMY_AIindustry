Chip Island and Cloud Enclave

Who pays for Taiwan’s and Malaysia’s national technological spaces?

Gan Sin Tiong and Wu Yun-Ching Paper presented at ACSEAST 2026, the Annual Conference on Southeast Asian Studies in Taiwan (draft; please do not cite)

Most studies ask why technological catch-up in Taiwan and Malaysia turned out so differently. This page asks another question: the land, water and power that catch-up depends on, from which sectors, places and groups were they drawn? The map moves with the paper’s three phases, from the export processing zones of the 1960s to today’s power plants and the Johor Strait.

Introduction

From “did catch-up succeed?” to “who pays?”

Fabs and data centers are the part we see above ground. This study is about what lies beneath: where did the land for science parks come from? Through which institutions, and from whom, were the water and power that fabs and server farms need drawn?

Using the concept of national technological space, we compare the two countries over thirty years: how what gets externalized expanded from labor and land to water and power.

Scene 1 · Taiwan, 2021

Irrigation suspended on over 74,000 hectares

In what was called the worst drought in a century, the government suspended irrigation for the first rice crop on a large scale across Taiwan, in the irrigation districts of Taoyuan, Hsinchu and Miaoli, Taichung, and Chiayi-Tainan: about 24% of all irrigated area. In the same period, TSMC and other chipmakers trucked in water to keep production going.

When the south faced drought again two years later, international media reported that rice farmers there had been unable to plant for a third year in a row.

Circles on the map show the rough location of the irrigation districts where water was cut off.

Sources: Central News Agency (2021); Feng (2023)

Scene 2 · Johor, 2025

Why approvals stopped: about 200 times the water

In November 2025, the Johor state government announced that it would no longer approve Tier 1 and Tier 2 data centers, because such facilities use about 200 times as much water as Tier 3 and Tier 4. New data centers must use recycled water and be concentrated in designated industrial areas.

The two restrictions came at core nodes of global semiconductor manufacturing and of AI cloud infrastructure. They are not isolated accidents but one question: when the state goes all out to produce dedicated space for technological capital, who bears the cost?

Source: New Straits Times (2025)

Prelude · 1960s–1980s

Both countries started from enclaves

By the 1960s, American electronics firms had moved into the export processing zones of southern Taiwan for packaging, testing and assembly. In 1972 Penang’s Bayan Lepas Free Trade Zone opened, and Clarion and National Semiconductor began production right away.

What was cheapened then was labor. In the 1970s, all direct production jobs in Malaysia’s semiconductor plants were done by women, each bonding machine run by one woman under close supervision; Taiwan had its home-based subcontracting system of “living rooms as factories”.

Sources: Amsden & Chu (2003); Rasiah (2010); Ong (1987); Hsiung (1996)

Prelude to Phase 1 · Taiwan

Set up the park first, buy the land later

Hsinchu Science Park (established in 1980; a prelude in this paper) was set up under a statute on science-based industrial parks promulgated in 1979. It was established first as an administrative entity, and the land was bought afterwards. Firms that moved in paid below-market rent for factory buildings and sites: the land itself was part of the state subsidy.

From the mid-1990s the Hsinchu model was copied in Tainan: this is Taiwan’s Phase 1. Expanding science parks meant the state had to keep turning farmland into industrial land, through farmland conversion, floating zoning and zone expropriation.

Sources: Mathews (1997); Amsden & Chu (2003)

Phase 1 · Malaysia

Cyberjaya, built on an oil palm estate

The Multimedia Super Corridor was launched in the mid-1990s. Its core city, Cyberjaya, was built on the former Prang Besar oil palm estate owned by the government-linked company Golden Hope, and opened in 1997. Indian estate workers whose families had lived there for generations were forced to move.

Plantations are a legacy of the colonial economy, and also a land bank that the postcolonial state and government-linked companies can deploy directly. With the idea of differential expropriability, the paper asks: which land, and which communities, are most easily marked for taking?

Sources: Bunnell (2004); Gan (2021b); Edwards & Jomo (1993)

Phase 2 · 2000s–2010s

For the first time, water joins land as a public dispute

Phase 4 of the Central Taiwan Science Park, the Erlin Park, was planned to need 155,000 tonnes of industrial water a day. In August 2011, hundreds of Changhua farmers and activists rallied at a sluice gate in Xizhou Township against plans to draw water for the park from the Cizaipi Canal, which is fed by the Zhuoshui River; the diversion plan was for over 65,000 tonnes a day.

Water joined land as a key cost of technological space, and those bearing it expanded from landowners and residents in expropriation zones to the farmers of a whole irrigation district and the coastal ecology. In the same period, Malaysia’s state-owned foundry Silterra began production in Kulim in 2000, and stayed at mature process technologies for many years.

The dashed line on the map shows the direction of the diversion, schematically.

Sources: Business Today Taiwan (2010); Taipei Times (2011); Rasiah (2015); Lee & Hutchinson (2026)

Phase 3 · Taiwan

Water moves to industry; power is tied to power plants

In 2020, Taiwan’s irrigation associations were turned into government agencies. After the 2021 suspension, the government promoted large-zone rotation: about 26,000 to 31,000 hectares rotate out of rice each year. If every farmer declared a switch of crops, this would save 180 to 220 million tonnes of water a year, “equal to one Shimen Reservoir”.

In August 2024, Taipower said it would no longer supply data centers of 5MW or more north of Taoyuan, and the Ministry of Economic Affairs said bluntly: “If you want the big AI companies, please support Taipower’s power plants.” In July 2026, Taipower named the areas around the Tatan and Hsieh-ho power plants as places where AI data center zones could be planned. Technological space began to be tied directly to power plants (infrastructure anchoring).

Sources: Central News Agency (2020); Central News Agency (2021); TechNews (2024a); United Daily News (2026)

Phase 3 · Malaysia

Singapore’s demand crosses the Johor Strait

Singapore approved no new data centers from 2019 and began lifting the pause only in 2022, so demand moved north to Johor. DC Byte estimated that Johor’s capacity grew from 10MW in early 2021 to 1,500MW in 2024, but that includes capacity under construction and planned; capacity actually operating in July 2025 was 487MW.

The state used TNB’s “Green Lane” to cut the time to get power connected from 36–48 months to 12 months, and set up the Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone in 2025. Cross-border water was already there: under the 1962 agreement, Singapore may draw 250 million gallons of raw water a day from the Johor River. Domestic capital is involved too: YTL Power’s data center with NVIDIA in Kulai is promoted as “sovereign AI”.

The dashed line shows demand moving north; the solid line, raw water flowing south (schematic).

Sources: W.Media (2024); DatacenterDynamics (2025); Tenaga Nasional Berhad (2023); Ministry of Foreign Affairs Singapore (n.d.); Kadir, Andri & Partners (2025)

Diverging paths

State
Johor: no more Tier 1 and Tier 2 data centersSelangor: water tariff for data centersState approval of land

Under the Federal Constitution, water resources are a state matter, and land acquisition needs state approval. So it was Johor that stopped approving data centers, and Selangor that set a dedicated water tariff for them (RM5.31 per cubic meter, against RM0.65 in the lowest household band).

In Taiwan’s unitary state, by contrast, the central government both courts investment and allocates water and power: an important institutional condition behind the two countries’ diverging paths of externalization.

Sources: Piarapakaran (n.d.); Kadir, Andri & Partners (2025); New Straits Times (2025); Malay Mail (2025); DatacenterDynamics (2025); Tenaga Nasional Berhad (2023); Tenaga Nasional Berhad (2025)

Land from space · Taiwan

In 16 years, 53,000 hectares of farmland vanished from satellite view

The University of Maryland mapped the world’s cropland from Landsat imagery, in cells 30 meters square. We compared the 2003 and 2019 maps and marked the cells that were cropland before and no longer are.

  • Green: cropland still farmed in 2019
  • Orange: cropland in 2003, no longer in 2019
  • Red: the same, and built over with houses, factories or roads by 2021

Together that is about 53,000 hectares, roughly twice the size of Taipei City. About 11,000 hectares of it (about a fifth, roughly the size of Hsinchu City) has been built over; the rest may be fallow, planted with fruit trees, or still empty.

Over the same period, about 40,000 hectares were newly mapped as cropland (for example, land farmed again after a long fallow), so the net loss is about 13,000 hectares (−3.8%). The Ministry of Agriculture’s statistics also show a decline: Taiwan’s cultivated land fell from about 803,000 hectares in 2012 to about 775,000 hectares in 2025 (−3.4%). The two cover different years and are calculated differently, so only the broad trend can be compared.

Note: this satellite map counts only herbaceous crops such as rice and vegetables, not orchards or tea. The colors show only that land use changed, not that science parks used all of it.

Land from space · Taichung

Taichung lost 17%, and the red clusters around the science park

Zoom in to Taichung. From 2003 to 2019, the cropland visible from space in Taichung City shrank from about 24,000 to 20,000 hectares, down 17%: the largest drop among counties and cities with more than 10,000 hectares of cropland. About 40% of the lost cropland has been built over, also the highest of any county or city.

The largest patch of red lies in the Taichung Park of the Central Taiwan Science Park (established in 2003): within the park boundary marked on OpenStreetMap, about 70% of the land was still cropland in 2003. At the Qixing site of the Houli Park to the north, the share is about 80%.

Red dots are also scattered around the park: new roads, housing or factories. Satellites cannot tell which land the park used directly and which went to development the park brought with it.

The dashed frames are park boundaries (from OpenStreetMap).

Land from space · Chiayi, 2025

Packaging plants for AI chips, built on farmland

This is satellite imagery from 2025; the dashed frame is the area of the Chiayi Science Park then under construction. TSMC is building CoWoS and other advanced packaging plants here: demand for AI chips from NVIDIA, AMD and others has soared, and advanced packaging capacity cannot keep up.

The green laid on top marks land that satellites still classified as cropland in 2019: about 90% of this site was cropland then. Construction began in May 2024. Work on the first plant stopped after suspected archaeological remains were found, so TSMC rushed the second plant ahead; by July 2026 the first plant to be finished was in mass production. The science park’s second phase to the east, about 90 hectares, broke ground the same month and will hold two more packaging plants.

  • Green: cropland still farmed in 2019
  • Dashed frame: Chiayi Science Park (2025 construction area and phase 2)

Land from space · Malaysia

Johor’s oil palm area fell 13% in five years

The satellite cropland map above counts only herbaceous crops (in Malaysia, mostly rice), not oil palm, a tree that grows for more than twenty years once planted. So here we turn to the planted-area statistics of the Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB).

From 2019 to 2024, Malaysia’s oil palm planted area fell by about 287,000 hectares (−4.9%). Johor, where data centers are most concentrated, lost about 99,000 hectares (−13%), more than the whole of Singapore; Selangor lost 21% and Perak 14%.

Not every lost estate became a data center, though: land may also go to other crops, housing or industrial estates, and the figures rise and fall from year to year. Johor’s was back up to about 676,000 hectares in 2025. The plantation group SD Guthrie has said it will set aside about 10,000 hectares over the next ten years (starting with old rubber estates and low-yielding oil palm land) for solar farms and industrial parks.

Each circle’s area equals the oil palm area the state lost from 2019 to 2024; circles sit at rough state centers.

Land from space · Kulai, Johor, 2025

2025: the oil palm estate has become a data center

The same place in 2025 satellite imagery. In August 2022, YTL and Singapore’s Sea group broke ground here on the park’s first phase; the whole park is planned for 500MW. Large server hall roofs and cleared, graded land are already visible.

The computing facility that YTL Power is building with NVIDIA under the banner of “sovereign AI”, mentioned earlier, is in this park.

This is one concrete case of oil palm estates becoming data centers in Johor; how many estates across the state have been converted this way can only be known by checking them one by one.

Actual load in December 2024 was only 405MW, about 21% of connected capacity; in June 2025, the actual load of all data centers was 603MW, about 47% of declared maximum demand. What the state has committed may well be reserved capacity: an externalization that comes before actual use and is harder to see. How these investments enter the tariff base, and who pays for them, remains to be examined.

The regulatory turn after 2024

Re-internalization, or spatial relocation?

The test is who bears the cost, and where.

Re-internalization

  • Taiwan: electricity prices for data centers raised by 15% to 25% (2024)
  • Taiwan: water consumption fee for large water users (2023)
  • Malaysia: RP4 tariffs itemized by cost component (2025)
  • Selangor: dedicated water tariff for data centers (2025)

Spatial relocation

  • Taiwan: data centers pushed toward the Tatan and Hsieh-ho power plants
  • Malaysia: data centers pushed toward recycled water and renewable energy
  • To be tested: whether plantations are being turned into solar farms

The paper therefore revises H3: partial, price-based re-internalization coexists with spatial relocation.

Much of the difference between the two countries may come from industry type and scale rather than from the relationship between state and capital itself; Johor’s position next to Singapore is a geographic scope condition. The cross-border externalization of water is mainly the historical structure of the 1962 agreement, to which data centers so far add only at the margin; the cross-border spillover that holds up better is likely in land and grid capacity.

Conclusion

The other side of state capacity

State capacity is not only about bearing the risks of technological catch-up; it also includes the capacity to marshal land, water and the grid. The other side of that capacity is the continual shifting of costs onto particular communities and geographic peripheries.

When server farms and solar panels replace oil palm estates and rice fields, who loses the land, and who bears the cost of the water and power?

The next phase of fieldwork will be based in Johor and in the farming and fishing villages of central and southern Taiwan:

  • Tracking the gap between contracted maximum demand and actual load over time
  • The situation of estate workers as Johor’s plantations turn into data centers and solar farms
  • Compiling cadastral and archival records of farmland expropriation and conversion for Taiwan’s science parks
